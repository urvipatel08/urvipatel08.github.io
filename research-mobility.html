Mobility for Life: From Car-Centric to Human-Centric Streets

MUD 602 History of Urban Design · Thomas Jefferson University · Philadelphia, 2025 · Urvi Patel

Streets are the most significant yet most overlooked public spaces in cities. This paper argues that future cities must transform streets from simple connectors between two destinations to the destination itself, addressing the economic, design, and environmental crises created by car-centric planning.

Introduction

Before we started to prioritise cars, for most of urban history streets were the most significant public spaces in the city. Where public life was visible, shared, and democratic. Before the dominance of automobiles, streets were flexible, multifunctional public rooms.

But 20th century planning replaced these social environments with what Andre Gorz calls the "car-ocracy." A city shaped around the private vehicle. Zoning, traffic engineering, and hierarchical road design transformed streets into channels of speed rather than places of life. Le Corbusier imagined the city as a "machine", prioritizing traffic flow over human experience, while Radburn's street hierarchy pushed pedestrians to leftover spaces. The result: a mobility system designed not for people, but for cars.

Jan Gehl later observed that we redesigned cities "for the scale of the car, not for the scale of the human." These concerns are not nostalgic; they reveal an urgent truth that if streets fail, the city fails.

Central Argument

This paper argues that future cities must transform streets from simple connectors between two destinations to the destination itself. It is not about banning cars from the streets, but about giving the choice back to humans. Comfort in everyday movement. And to rebuild the ecosystem of mobility, we must address three intertwined problems.

Cities like Copenhagen prove that the old logic can still return and we can still reclaim our streets. Bogota's Ciclovia opens major streets as public space every Sunday. Seoul restored a buried river after removing a highway. All these examples prove that streets can once again become destinations.

Problem OneThe Economic Pressure That Forces Car Dependency

The dependency on cars for everyday work is not totally a personal preference but also a result of policy, geometry, and economics that force us to choose cars. Transportation planning often optimizes streets for "rush hours" but leaves out the major part of shift workers, caregivers, youth, elders, and low-income families who travel outside these windows and rely most on the transportation modes that are being ignored.

$9,000 The AAA estimates the annual cost of car ownership is $9,000 to $12,000 per year, which is higher than rent in many cities. People don't choose cars because they want to; car-centric design gives them no other option. Gorz calls this "auto-obligatory mobility" — movement performed out of obligation, not freedom.

Parking infrastructure worsens all these problems. Some cities like Phoenix and Houston give more land to parking than they allocate for housing or any other public spaces. This leads to higher rents, disconnected neighbourhoods, heat islands, and unwalkable areas. Reducing this dependency on cars and giving people their choices back is not just about mobility but also about economic justice.

In Groningen, Netherlands they reorganized the centre into four cells which allow cars to enter in the city but not pass through. This made the choice of walking, cycling, and transit easier and more convenient. Traffic evaporated not because people abandoned cars, but because the street system no longer rewarded driving. The dependency on cars is not natural but it is designed by us only and it can be redesigned.

"People don't choose cars because they want to; they do so because cities are designed to give them no other option."
Andre Gorz

Problem TwoThe Necessity to Prioritise Human Comfort and Choices in Design

A human-centred city is one that prioritizes human comfort, gives people choice, and focuses on keeping the pace at human speed. As Jan Gehl describes, designing for 5 km/h — the pace of walking. At 50 km/h nothing exists, but at slower speeds people notice details and interact with others. Design elements like short blocks, narrow lanes, clear crossings, seating, and active edges are not just ornaments — they shape human behaviour.

Jane Jacobs argued that safety comes from life, not from enforcement. Safety becomes organic when there are "Eyes on the Street" — when streets are active, mixed, and engaging. David Sim's Soft City furthers this; it approaches flexibility with places where built form and public life support each other.

European cities are the best example for human-scale urban development. Copenhagen's bicycling culture is not from moral persuasion but from systematic infrastructure decisions made over decades. When we see Copenhagen's bicycling culture, it is not just from moral persuasion but from intentional, cumulative design decisions made across generations.

Barcelona

Spain — Superblocks

Clustering blocks into larger units and restricting through-traffic reclaims interior streets as pedestrian space. Human-scale design transforms neighbourhoods within an existing urban grid.

Paris

France — 15-Minute City

Everyday needs accessible within 15 minutes by foot or bicycle. School streets, car-free zones, and repurposed parking have transformed a car-oriented capital into a model of walkability.

Bogota

Colombia — Ciclovia

76 miles of streets open to cyclists and pedestrians every Sunday since 1974. When people are given space, they give life back. Now replicated from Los Angeles to Jakarta.

Times Square

New York, USA

Redesigned as a pedestrian plaza: 40% fewer pedestrian injuries, 20% decrease in crime, 60% decrease in pollution, 80% of visitors feel safer. 40 million visitors annually.

"People naturally walk when distances are short, the routes are interesting, and the environment feels safe."
Jan Gehl — Cities for People (2010)

Problem ThreeThe Environmental and Health Harm of Auto-Centric Mobility

Car-centric cities generate crises far beyond tailpipe emissions. Noise pollution causes stress, hypertension, and sleep disruption. PM2.5 particulate matter from traffic exceeds WHO guidelines in residential streets near heavy corridors. Asphalt-covered streets create urban heat islands 20 to 30 degrees Fahrenheit hotter than shaded streets. Vehicle waste heat accounts for up to 30% of anthropogenic heat emissions in cities — the second-largest source after buildings.

Electric vehicles that are highly promoted as the future reduce tailpipe emissions but do not address other negative externalities like congestion, noise from tire friction, dangerous high-speed environments, and land consumption. More importantly the production of EV batteries is resource-intensive, requiring lithium, cobalt, nickel, and manganese. All these processes release massive carbon emissions before the car even reaches the road. To improve health, it is not an option to simply electrify — we need to reduce the dependence.

ConclusionStreets as Destinations

No space is more ordinary or more overlooked than streets and to make them truly liveable we do not need grand architectural gestures. When we move streets from only channels for vehicles to reclaim them as places for people, they become more joyful, equitable, and sustainable.

It is clear from the history that when we design a car-centric city, it produces spatial inefficiency, economic burden, social isolation, and environmental harm. Our current mobility system over-prioritizes vehicles at the expense of human comfort. And yet, the solutions are clear. Removing or even just reducing car dependency returns streets back to their older functions and encourages walking and transit use.

Cities like Groningen, Copenhagen, Bogota, and Paris prove that transformation is not only possible but already happening. When we design for people first, behaviour shifts naturally, streets become more calmer, safer, and more economically resilient. Children gain independence, local businesses thrive, public life returns, environment improves and the whole city begins to feel human again.

It is not just about technology, eliminating cars or design strategy but it is also a cultural shift, a political stance, and an ethical responsibility. Our efforts to change the way we design will shape the future of our cities. It is not just simply about shifting from car-centric to human-centric but it is about shifting towards better possibilities for our future cities.
