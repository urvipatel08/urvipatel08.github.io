MUD 602 — History of Urban Design · Thomas Jefferson University · 2025

Mobility for Life: From Car-Centric to Human-Centric Streets

Streets are the first and most important — yet most underrated — public spaces in urban development. For too long they have been designed as corridors of speed rather than corridors of life.

Central Argument

Future cities must transform streets from simple connectors between two destinations to the destination itself. It is not about banning cars — it is about giving the choice back to humans, measuring distance not in miles per hour but in moments per block.

Three Intertwined Problems
01 — Economic

The Economic Pressure That Forces Car Dependency

Car dependency is not a personal preference — it is the result of policy, geometry, and economics that remove any other viable choice. Transportation planning optimized for "rush hours" ignores shift workers, caregivers, youth, elders, and low-income families who travel outside those windows.

$9,000–12,000 Annual cost of car ownership (AAA) — higher than rent in many US cities. Gorz calls this "auto-obligatory mobility": movement performed out of obligation, not freedom.
02 — Design

The Necessity to Prioritise Human Comfort and Choice

A human-centred city designs for 5 km/h — the pace of walking. At 50 km/h nothing exists; at slower speeds people notice details, interact, and participate in public life. Short blocks, narrow lanes, clear crossings, seating, and active edges are not ornaments: they shape human behaviour.

5 km/h Jan Gehl's design speed for human-centred cities. Safety becomes organic when streets are active and mixed — "Eyes on the Street" (Jacobs), not enforcement.
03 — Environment

The Environmental and Health Harm of Auto-Centric Mobility

Car-centric cities generate crises far beyond emissions. Noise pollution causes stress, hypertension, and sleep disruption. PM2.5 particulate matter from traffic exceeds WHO guidelines in residential streets near heavy corridors, increasing asthma and cognitive impairment in children.

20–30°F Hotter: asphalt-covered streets versus shaded streets. Vehicle waste heat accounts for up to 30% of anthropogenic heat emissions in cities — the second-largest source after buildings.
40% Fewer pedestrian injuries after Times Square, NYC was redesigned as a pedestrian plaza
93% Of visitors agree Times Square is now a more pleasant place — 40 million people pass through annually
1/3 Of land in some cities dedicated to storing empty cars — Phoenix and Houston give more land to parking than to housing
"People naturally walk when the distances are short, the routes are interesting, and the environment feels safe."
Jan Gehl — Cities for People (2010)
Global Case Studies
Groningen Netherlands

Reorganised the city centre into four traffic cells that allow cars to enter but not pass through. Walking, cycling, and transit became easier and more convenient. Traffic evaporated — not because people abandoned cars, but because the street system no longer rewarded driving.

Result — Car dependency is designed, and it can be redesigned.
Copenhagen Denmark

Wasn't always walkable — in the 1960s it was congested and car-heavy. Through decades of systematic design — protected lanes, slow intersections, and unified networks — the city built a culture of everyday cycling not through moral persuasion but through infrastructure.

Result — Design creates culture, not the other way around.
Paris France

The 15-minute city model ensures that everyday needs — work, school, groceries, parks — are accessible within 15 minutes by foot or bicycle. School streets, car-free zones, and repurposed parking have transformed a car-oriented capital into a model of comfort and walkability.

Result — When everyday needs are close, car dependence naturally shrinks.
Barcelona Spain

Superblocks cluster city blocks into larger units and restrict through-traffic, reclaiming interior streets as public space for pedestrians and cyclists. The intervention demonstrates that human-scale design can create calm, liveable neighbourhoods within an existing urban grid.

Result — Infrastructure at block scale changes neighbourhoods at city scale.
Seoul South Korea

Removed the Cheonggye Expressway — an elevated urban highway — and restored the buried Cheonggyecheon stream beneath it. The transformation created a 5.8 km linear park through the heart of the city, demonstrating that infrastructure removal can generate public space and ecological restoration simultaneously.

Result — Dismantling car infrastructure can restore both nature and public life.
Bogotá Colombia

The Ciclovía opens 76 miles of streets to cyclists, pedestrians, and community activity every Sunday. Begun in 1974, it has become a weekly institution proving that when people are given space, they give life back. Cities from Los Angeles to Jakarta have adopted variations of the model.

"When you give people space, they give life back."
"We redesigned cities for the scale of the car, not for the scale of the human. If streets fail, the city fails."
Jan Gehl
Key Theoretical Voices
Jan Gehl

Cities for People

Argues for designing at the human scale — 5 km/h, not 50 km/h. His framework establishes that walkability is not a luxury but a basic human need: short distances, interesting routes, safe environments. His observations form the foundation of the human-centred streets movement.

Jane Jacobs

The Death and Life of Great American Cities

Established that street safety comes from life, not enforcement. Her concept of "Eyes on the Street" argues that mixed-use, active, and engaged streets generate organic safety — as opposed to the isolated, single-use corridors that modernist planning produced.

André Gorz

The "Car-ocracy"

Named the political economy of car dominance: a city shaped around the private vehicle in which zoning, traffic engineering, and road design transformed streets into channels of speed. His concept of "auto-obligatory mobility" describes movement performed out of obligation — not freedom — when no other option exists.

Conclusion

This is not just about technology, eliminating cars, or design strategy. It is a cultural shift, a political stance, and an ethical responsibility. When we design for people first, behaviour shifts naturally — streets become calmer, safer, and more economically resilient. The future city is not car-free; it is human-first.
