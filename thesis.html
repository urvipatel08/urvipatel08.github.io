Master's Thesis · In Progress

Extending the Node-Place Model for TOD

Integrating social equity and pedestrian environment quality: a case study of Philadelphia.

Thomas Jefferson University Master of Urban Design, Future Cities Advisor: Dr. Peng Du 2026
Transit-Oriented Development Node-Place Model Pedestrian Environment Social Equity GIS

Overview

Transit-oriented development is meant to channel growth into compact, walkable, mixed-use neighbourhoods around transit. In many American cities that has not happened. Some stations with strong service sit in areas that remain economically depressed, physically isolated, and short on pedestrian amenities.

This thesis treats that gap as a design and planning problem rather than a market problem, and one that falls hardest on the communities who depend on transit most.

The node-place model describes a station as both a node of connectivity and a place of land use. Later work added design quality and social vulnerability. The pedestrian environment, meaning the streets, crossings, and walking network between homes and stations, is still under-researched even though walking is the main way people reach transit. This research brings the four dimensions together and gives them equal weight.

What it asks

  • Which areas around Philadelphia's SEPTA stations have strong transit access but low development activity?
  • How do land use, economic, social, and pedestrian conditions differ around those stations?
  • Which three to five underperforming station areas deserve a closer urban design site analysis?
  • What site-specific design framework could activate them?

Method

A two-stage investigation. Stage 1 uses GIS-based spatial analysis to score every SEPTA fixed-rail station area in Philadelphia on four equally weighted dimensions. Stage 2 is an in-depth urban design site analysis of the selected stations, identifying physical and design conditions that block development and producing site-specific recommendations.

Four dimensions, twenty variables

Five measurable variables sit under each dimension, drawn from SEPTA GTFS, the U.S. Census, Philadelphia open data, OpenStreetMap, and Vision Zero.

Node

Transit connectivity · SEPTA GTFS

  • Number of transit lines
  • Service frequency
  • Multimodal and transfer connectivity
  • Destinations reachable within 30 minutes
  • Station accessibility

Place

Land use and economy · Census, city open data

  • Population density
  • Employment density
  • Land-use mix
  • Destination accessibility
  • Vacancy rate

Social

Vulnerability and equity · Census ACS

  • Median household income
  • Car ownership rate
  • Racial composition
  • Elderly population
  • Renter proportion

Pedestrian Network

Walking environment · OpenStreetMap, city data, Vision Zero

  • Pedshed ratio
  • Intersection density
  • Sidewalk coverage
  • Crossing infrastructure
  • Physical barriers

Analysis

All twenty variables are standardised to a 0 to 1 range and combined with entropy weighting, so variables that differ more across stations carry more weight. Each station area receives four composite scores.

K-means clustering groups the station areas. The number of clusters is chosen with the elbow method and tested for stability across 1,000 trials. Areas with high node scores but low place, social, and pedestrian scores are flagged as underperforming and move to Stage 2.

Where it stands

GTFS data for all 13 fixed-rail route identifiers has been collected and analysed, and half-mile catchment buffers have been built in ArcGIS Pro for every fixed-rail station inside the city limits. The datasets for the remaining fifteen variables have been identified and confirmed available.

Early mapping shows three patterns. The strongest node values concentrate in the inner city. Large parts of outer Philadelphia, including the Northeast, Far Northeast, and outer Southwest, have no walkable fixed-rail access and fall outside the study. And along well-served inner corridors, neighbouring stations differ sharply: some sit in active mixed-use settings while others, with equally good transit, are surrounded by largely empty streets. That contrast is the research condition this thesis sets out to explain.

North Philadelphia, where high-speed rail and Regional Rail come together, is the most viable candidate for site analysis so far.
