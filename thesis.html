Extending the Node-Place Model

A four-dimensional framework for identifying underperforming transit stations in Philadelphia.

Overview

Transit alone doesn't build a neighbourhood.

Transit-oriented development planning has long relied on Luca Bertolini's node-place model (1999), which plots stations on two axes: their value as a node in the transport network, and the intensity of the place around them. Where the two are balanced, a station is considered healthy. Where they diverge, it is flagged for attention.

Two dimensions flatten much of what decides whether a station area actually works. A station can have excellent service and a reasonable mix of land uses, yet be hostile to walk through, or serve a community with little access to opportunity. This thesis extends the model from two dimensions to four, adding Pedestrian and Social, and applies it to all 50 rapid-transit stations on Philadelphia's SEPTA network.

The purpose is diagnostic: to find the stations where transit investment is already strong but the surroundings, walkability, or community conditions have not kept pace. Those are the places where urban design can do the most good, because the hardest and most expensive piece, the rail line, is already in the ground.

The problem

The classic model asks one question: is a station's place as developed as its transit? That framing hides two things that shape how a station area performs on the ground.

The walk is folded into "place"

Whether people can reach the station safely and comfortably on foot is treated as part of place, even though good sidewalks and a fine street grid are a different thing from density and destinations.

The community is missing

Who lives around the station, and whether they have access to opportunity, is absent entirely, even though equity is central to the case for public transit.

Research question

Can a four-dimensional extension of the node-place model, adding a Pedestrian and a Social dimension, identify stations whose surroundings underperform relative to their transit value, in ways the two-dimensional model cannot reveal?

Earlier studies extended the model one axis at a time. What's new here is bringing walkability and social equity into a single four-part measure. Each dimension is built from five variables, twenty in all.

Node

The station's value in the transit network

  • Transit lines served
  • Service frequency (peak)
  • Transit stops within the catchment
  • Peak to off-peak service ratio
  • Service span (hours of operation)

Place

The intensity and mix of activity around it

  • Population density
  • Employment density
  • Land-use mix (entropy index)
  • Destination access (points of interest)
  • Vacancy reversed

Pedestrian

The quality of the walking environment

  • Pedshed (share of the catchment reachable on foot)
  • Intersection density
  • Pedestrian street quality (sidewalk width)
  • Crossing safety (High-Injury Network exposure)
  • Physical barriers reversed

Social

Equity and access to opportunity

  • Median income
  • Households without a car reversed
  • Minority population share reversed
  • Elderly population share reversed
  • Renter share reversed

Reversed variables are flipped before scoring so that a higher value is always the "better" direction. On the Social dimension this means a lower score marks a community with greater need: more households without a car, lower incomes, more elderly residents. It lets the framework flag where strong transit is serving, or failing to serve, the people who depend on it most.

Why Philadelphia

Philadelphia's dense rail network was built for a much larger industrial population. Transit is strong, but many station areas were left behind as the city changed, and income and safety disparities are often sharpest exactly where transit is strongest.

The study covers 50 stations on the city's two rapid-transit lines: the Broad Street Line, the north-south subway (24 stations), and the Market-Frankford Line, the east-west line that runs elevated through much of the city (26 stations). Each is analysed within a half-mile catchment, the distance generally treated as a comfortable walk to transit. Regional Rail and the Norristown High-Speed Line are outside the scope.

Method

The scoring is deliberately simple and transparent, so the results can be read and questioned.

  1. MeasureDoneTwenty variables per station, from SEPTA service data, the U.S. Census and American Community Survey, OpenStreetMap and municipal GIS layers, the city's Complete Streets and High-Injury Network data, and points-of-interest data.
  2. NormaliseDoneEvery variable is scaled from 0 to 1 using min-max normalisation. Six variables are reversed first so that higher is always better.
  3. CombineDoneEqual weighting at both levels: five variables are averaged into each dimension score, and the four dimension scores are averaged into one overall TOD score.
  4. DiagnoseDoneStations are ranked on every dimension, and four case stations are chosen to span the range from healthiest to weakest.
  5. Site analysis and field studyNextOn-the-ground study of the physical and design conditions holding the underperforming stations back.
  6. Design proposalsNextTargeted urban design proposals matched to each station's diagnosis.

Why equal weights? Equal weighting is a transparent default supported in the TOD-index literature. It avoids building contestable assumptions about which factors "matter more" into what is meant to be a diagnostic tool, and it keeps any one measure from taking over.

Why it matters

The four-dimensional extension separates problems the two-axis view blurs together, and surfaces underperformance that a strong node would otherwise mask. A station like Erie, with strong transit, a hollow place, and a community in need, is exactly where a city should prioritise investment: the expensive infrastructure is already there and the need is high.

Isolating Pedestrian and Social also lets each station be matched to the right kind of intervention.

What's next

The next stage moves from the map to the street: site analysis and field study of the underperforming stations, followed by targeted urban design proposals matched to each one's diagnosis. The thesis completes in December 2026.
